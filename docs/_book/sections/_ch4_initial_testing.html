6  Goal

We are trying to answer the following questions in this experiment: - Can an inverter at say, house 15, raise its voltage from (e.g., 0.92) up toward a target value (1.0 p.u.)? - If house 15 injects Q, how much do the voltages at the rest of the houses change? - How much reactive power is necessary to achieve a particular household voltage target? Is this amount within the inverter capability?

6.1 Setup

The GridLAB-D file is set to 15 houses, with a single substation transformer and distribution transformer. There are two nodes: upstream node and node 4. The upstream node is a swing bus node. Node 4 is the main node wherein all the loads connect to.

6.1.1 Initial Case

None of the houses have inverters in them. The voltagge behavior is depicted at each house, as well as the source node.

Note that the voltage drops as the house is at a further distance from node 4.

6.1.2 Sweep the voltage at Node 4

When the voltage is swept at Node 4, the same voltage drop is depicted at different houses location.

Figure 6.1 demonstrates that the household voltage response depends on both the upstream voltage and the household’s location along the feeder. Note that the distance of voltage lines in Figure 6.1 decreases consecutively. The current flow in the line supplying house 1 carries the combined current of many downstream loads. The line between house 1 and house 2 carries slightly less current, and so forth. From ohm’s law:

\[\Delta \bar{\mathbf{V}} = \bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{I}}\]

If the line segments approximately equal impedances, the voltage drop across each segment generally decreases as we move downstream because the current decreases. Therefore:

\[|\Delta \bar{\mathbf{V}}_{1,2}| > |\Delta \bar{\mathbf{V}}_{13,14}| > |\Delta \bar{\mathbf{V}}_{14,15}|\]

To explain this further, let’s look at all households at their lowest point, which is at 8:46 p.m.

The voltage behavior, as shown in Figure 6.2, is clearly nonlinear. And, the voltage drop distance between households decreases as we go down the node. This explains that the voltage trend is associated with the line impedance as well as the feeder loading, rather than a particular source voltage. This behavior is explain by:

\[\Delta \bar{\mathbf{V}} \approx \frac{RP+XQ}{\bar{\mathbf{V}}}\]

See the derivation of the above equation in the previous Section Equation 4.14.

6.1.3 Answering the first question

The question that we try to answer in this section is:

  • Can an inverter at say, house 15, raise its voltage from (e.g., 0.92) up toward a target value (1.0 p.u.)?

Not really. Figure 6.3 shows the behavior of a five kVA inverter installed at house 15. The inverter kept providing real power the whole day, resulting in \(V_{max} = 0.98\). The constant injection of \(Q\) is due to the fact that the target voltage, \(V_{target} = 1.00~p.u.\) was never reached. Note that there is a slight change inthe head of the feeder (Node 4 at \(2400~V\)). The change is almost zero. Also, the residential head (the secondary side of the transformer at \(120~V\)) sees a low voltage change as well, at \(0.006~V\).

Determining the relationship between Q and the Voltage

We already know the relationship between the voltage and reactive power. From the Jacobian submatrix, \[\bar{J}_{4} = \frac{\partial \bar{Q}}{\partial |V|}\]

Sweeping the reactive power (\(Q\)) from \(-5~kVAr\) to \(+5~kVAr\) on a \(5~kVA\) inverter at house 15 causes the voltage at that house to dropt first, because we are absorbing \(Q\) (\(-Q\)). Note that at the top plot of Figure 6.4 the house voltage goes from 0.88 p.u. to 0.92. However, doing the unreasonable thing and have that inverter inject reactive power (\(Q\)) all day long would improve the house15 voltage to 0.98 p.u., as shown in Figure 6.3.

The mid plot in Figure 6.4 shows the voltage at the distribution transformer in blue and the substation transformer in orange. The y-axis the voltage difference between the basecase and the \(Q\) sweep.

Note that the voltage deviation is worest at 20:46, so the Figure 6.4 is only running the simulation at that time.

The lower plot, showing the voltages at each house, as we sweep \(Q\) at the inverter at house 15. Note that the voltage difference between, for example, \(+Q = 5~kVAr\) and \(Q = 0~kVAr\) is larger at house 15 than at the first house. In fact, this difference decreases as we get closer to the head of the branch. Again, referring the Jacobian matrix, particularly, \(J_{4}\), which describes the voltage/Q relationship:

\[\frac{\partial \bar{Q}_{15}}{\partial |V_{i}|}~~~~\text{where,}~i~\in \{1,2,3,\cdots, 15\}\]

The U-shape curve of \(V-Q\) curve can be obtained with more inverters installed and a well-optimized \(Q\) at each inverter (I think).
